A workbook for self-directed projects

Project Planning Toolkit

For taking a project of your own from a hunch to something made, whether it runs for a weekend or a whole term. Find what you want to work on, write a brief, choose how to research it, plan the making, and look back at how you worked. Every step hands you a design move to try.

You leave with a brief, research methods, quick experiments, a plan, and a workbook to print.

Discover · 3 of 4

React

Forty positions from the Practice Playground deck, one at a time. Don't analyse them; mark your first reaction and move on. You're not choosing one. You're finding out what you react to.

Why sort statements that aren't yours? Reacting is quicker than inventing. Your angry and bin piles often say more about what you care about than your keep pile does.

You don't have to sort all 40. After ten or so, look at your piles, and stop whenever something stands out.

Discover · 4 of 4

What kind of designer are you?

The kind of designer you are is defined by what you choose to care about, and what you refuse to ignore. It shows in the materials you work through, the people you choose to work with and design for, and the values that guide what you make.

Here are twelve sets of choices, most of them pairs. For each set, tap the side you lean towards today. There's no right answer and no score. It's a snapshot of you at the start of this project, and you'll see it again at the very end, to find out what moved.

Go with your first answer. If you really can't choose, leave the pair blank: a blank is an answer too.

Brief · 1 of 2

Your brief

You'll rewrite this more than once, and that's expected. A brief is a tool for deciding, not a promise. Keep each version and you'll see how your thinking moved.

Five parts that work as one system, each shaping the others. Everything you do next should answer to this statement. Once you have it, decisions get quicker, even when things get messy.

    Brief · 2 of 2

    This week's challenge

    Before you go further, set yourself one small task for this week: something you wouldn't normally do, that moves your brief along. You'll bring back what it made, to your next check-in or to whoever you're working with.

    Small is the point. If it would take more than an afternoon, cut it in half.

    Methods · 1 of 2

    Research methods

    Why not do them straight away? Most methods need a place, a person or an hour you don't have right now. Writing them up first means you go out with a plan and come back with something to write down.

    Four short methods, one for each part of your brief. Answer one question for each: your answer chooses the move, and the words from your brief fill it in.

    You don't do them here. Write each one up, say when you'll do it and how long you'll give it, and add it to your methods. Print them as cards, go and do them, then come back and write what you found.

    Stuck at any point? The Stuck? button opens a list of what tends to go wrong in a project. Tick what's true and each one hands you a move. Use it whenever you need it, as often as you need it.
    Methods · 2 of 2

    What you found

    Come back here once you've done your methods. Write down only what surprised you; what you expected doesn't need writing down. A method that found nothing surprising still counts, so write that.

    Experiment

    What makes a try cheap? It takes under an hour, uses what's to hand, and you wouldn't mind throwing it away. If a try needs buying or booking, make a rougher one first.

    You don't need a question to start. Try things quickly and cheaply: change one thing, look, and write down one thing you noticed. Do a few. When one keeps pulling at you, sharpen it into a question further down.

    Sharpen one into a questionOnly when a try keeps pulling at you

    Why sharpen?

    Only sharpen a try that keeps pulling at you, and only when you want to. Write what you expect before you start: if you write it afterwards, the result can't surprise you.
    Not a lab. You don't need statistics. You need to know what you changed, what you kept the same, and what actually happened, written down while it's fresh.
    Plan · 1 of 4

    What success looks like

    A promising idea only matters if you can make it real. Plan starts with the end: how you'll know it worked, and how you'll know it didn't. Each decision comes with a move that keeps it honest.

    Plan · 2 of 4

    How it unfolds

    Why mark where you could stop? Things go wrong. A step you could stop at is a version you can always finish and show.

    The steps you'll take, in order, and what each one needs. Then mark the steps where you could stop and still have something to show. The page reads them back to you as one plan: what to get in place first, and your way home if something fails.

    Plan · 3 of 4

    Who can help

    “Not for this project” is a fine answer. The point is knowing who's there, so that when you need someone, you already know who to ask.

    This is a networking exercise as much as a plan. Write down everyone you know who touches your project, even loosely: people you know well, people you know a bit, people you only know of. For each one, ask yourself whether it makes sense to contact them now, later, or not for this project.

    Small task · Go through your messages from the last month and write down everyone who could help with any part of your project, not only the obvious ones. First names or initials are enough.

    Who to contact this week

    First names or initials only, and no phone numbers or emails. What you type stays in this browser and on your printout.

    Plan · 4 of 4

    Money and time

    What you can spend, and the day it ends. The parts you mark by hand every week (your time grid, your budget lines, your task list) are on the third page of your summary, ready to print.

    Time management. Most people underestimate time by half or more. If you think something takes two hours, plan for three.

    Looking back

    Start with your project: what you made, and whether it did what you promised. Then look at how you worked.

    What your moves acted on

    Every move acts on something: the material, how the parts are arranged, the setting around the work, the process, the people, or what you notice. Here are the moves you used while discovering and the ones you actually carried out in your methods and experiment, sorted by what they act on. A method counts once you've written what you found; a try counts once you've written what you noticed. The third column shows moves next to yours that you haven't tried.

    Moves to look into next

    Moves connect to each other: one leads on to another, or pairs with it, or can stand in for it. These are the ones closest to what you already did, that you haven't tried.

    The last step

    Your three principles

    You've made something, and you've seen how your way of working moved. Before the next project, write down what you want to keep. Don't start from a blank: each principle comes with a small task that finds it in what you already did.

    What kind of designer are you now?

    The same choices you made on the “What kind of designer” page, at the start. Tap where you lean now. The dashed box is where you were at the start; anything that moved is marked.

    Keep them where you'll see them. Your principles print at the end of your summary. Next time you start a project, read them before you spin.

It fills itself as you work through the other steps, so there is nothing to edit here.
